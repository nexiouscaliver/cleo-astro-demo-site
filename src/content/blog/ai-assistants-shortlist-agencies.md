B2B buyers are now turning to AI assistants like ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot as a research step before they ever fill in a contact form. They ask these tools to explain a category, name likely candidates, and sum up what each agency is known for. That single shift, moving part of the shortlist upstream into a chat window, is what every agency now has to design for.

We cannot attach a precise adoption figure to that shift right now. The research we would normally cite here, from sources like Gartner or Forrester on AI-assisted B2B purchasing, was not available to verify at the time of writing, and we would rather say that plainly than print a number we cannot stand behind. What follows is grounded in how the buying process itself has changed, which is verifiable without a survey stat attached to every sentence.

## An AI assistant compares agencies for the buyer before any conversation starts

An AI assistant answers a buyer's question by pulling information together from across the web and handing it back as one readable summary, not a list of links to click through. Ask it "who are the best B2B content marketing agencies in Singapore" and you won't get ten blue links back. It gives you a short, confident answer with three or four names already attached.

That is a different job than a search engine does. A search engine ranks pages and leaves the comparing to you. An AI assistant does some of that comparing itself, before you have spoken to a single agency.

For an agency like Harbourline Digital, that means the summary a prospective client receives may already contain a judgement about who is credible in a category, formed from whatever public content the system can find and trust. We pay close attention to that, because it happens whether or not we are in the room.

## Getting shortlisted now means being summarisable, not just findable

Getting shortlisted used to mean ranking well enough, or being referred often enough, that a buyer found you during their own search. Now it also means being the kind of source an AI assistant can confidently summarise and attach a name to.

That is a subtly different task. The two systems draw on different signals:

- A search engine ranks pages through backlinks and keyword targeting alone.
- An AI assistant tends to draw on pages that state things plainly: who the agency is, what it specialises in, what results it has delivered, in language that does not require much interpreting.
- Vague positioning statements and clever taglines do not extract well, because there is nothing concrete in them to extract.

We have watched this play out with our own clients' content. Pages built around a clear, specific claim, a named service, a named industry, a stated outcome, tend to get referenced far more usefully than pages built around brand tone alone.

## What buyers are actually asking an AI assistant

The questions a buyer types into an AI assistant tend to fall into a few recognisable patterns:

- Category questions: "who does B2B SEO for SaaS companies in Singapore"
- Comparison questions: "how does agency A differ from agency B"
- Validation questions: "is this agency credible, what do clients say about them"
- Scoping questions: "what should a B2B content retainer cost"

Each rewards a different kind of content. Category questions reward clear positioning on a page that states exactly what you do and for whom. Comparison and validation questions reward specific, checkable evidence, case studies, named results, client outcomes, rather than general claims about quality. An agency that publishes only broad statements about "driving growth" gives an AI assistant nothing concrete to repeat back to a buyer who asked a direct question.

## The AI summary, not the original research, is what reaches the buying committee

An AI assistant's summary often reaches more people inside the buying company than the original research ever did, simply because it's so easy to paste into a Slack message or forward in an email. The person doing the initial research and the person signing off on the agency are often different people, and it's usually the AI-generated summary that travels between them.

That is worth sitting with. A single well-extracted answer does not just shape one researcher's opinion. It becomes the shorthand the entire buying committee uses to discuss the shortlist, which raises the stakes on getting that summary right in the first place.

## What Harbourline Digital checks before recommending this approach to a client

Before we advise a client on showing up well in AI-assisted research, we look at their existing content the way an AI assistant would. Can a system pull a clear answer from this page in one pass, or does it have to guess? We treat this as a basic content audit, not something to bolt on later, because a page written only for a human skimmer can read as ambiguous to an AI system doing the same job at speed.

We also assess whether a client's strongest proof points are written in language specific enough to survive being summarised. That means checking:

- The case studies, whether they name a specific industry and a specific result
- The general success stories, which tend to become vague AI summaries when left unspecific
- The specific ones, with a named industry and a named result, which tend to travel intact

## The takeaway for agencies watching this shift

An agency doesn't need to chase every AI platform individually to benefit here. The discipline underneath it all hasn't changed: say plainly who you serve, what you're good at, and what you've delivered, in language that doesn't force a reader, or a machine, to guess the rest. B2B buyers using AI assistants to shortlist agencies are really just asking a quicker version of the same question that has always decided whether an agency gets picked.

The agencies that keep appearing in these summaries will not be the ones with the cleverest brand language. They will be the ones whose content answered the question before anyone had to ask it twice.

## FAQ

**Do AI assistants replace search engines for B2B buyers researching agencies?** Not entirely. Buyers still use traditional search, but a lot of them now start with an AI assistant first, using it to narrow a broad category down to a handful of names before checking further through search or referrals.

**Can an agency control what an AI assistant says about it?** Not directly. An agency can shape that by publishing clear, specific, checkable content about what it does and who it serves. That's the kind of content AI assistants pull from most reliably.

**Does this mean traditional SEO no longer matters?** No. AI assistants still lean heavily on the same crawlable, well-structured web content that search engines rank. So the groundwork, clear pages, clean structure, credible evidence, still counts.

**What should an agency's website prioritise for this kind of research?** Specific, concrete answers to the questions a buyer would actually type: who you serve, what you specialise in, and what results you've delivered, stated plainly rather than dressed up in brand language.
